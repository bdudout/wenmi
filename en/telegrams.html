Corpus

The telegrams, one by one

The seventeen 1916 telegrams read so far, on both channels: the letters as read on the forms, the resulting code and character, the confidence of each character, and, for doubtful passages, the errors that might explain them. At the end, in the same format, the condenser telegrams of March–May 1916, re-read on the scans, then twenty-six newly deciphered telegrams of April–June 1916.

Transcriptions by Brice Dudout (blind human readings); analysis with Claude (Anthropic).

How to read this page

Each telegram is given in three layers: the letters read on the Japanese copy (in groups of ten, as on the form), then, box by box, the two syllables, the telegraph code they give under the channel's key (G0 or Y0), and the corresponding Chinese character.

報 secure: a character expected in a text of this kind 粤 rare but confirmed (date on the form, word re-read on the image, name, graphic variant) 睟 doubtful: an unlikely character at this point · illegible, or syllable outside the grid

"Secure" means the character is plausible for the language model and consistent with the rest; it does not guarantee every letter. A "doubtful" character may also be right (a proper name, a rare technical term): it is a signal, not a verdict.

Where can errors come from?

Between Zhao Shen's text and our reading, every letter went through three stages, each with its typical errors:

  1. Morse transmission. Letters travel as dots and dashes. A lost, added or inverted sign changes the letter: e (·) becomes i (··), a (·−) becomes e (·) or w (·−−), and so on.
  2. Japanese copying. The clerk copies by hand words that mean nothing to him. Some letters in his handwriting get confused: g/q, x/d, u/n, a/o, k/h, f/b… (pairs noted on the images during the investigation).
  3. Our reading. The same confusions come into play a second time, a hundred and ten years later, on the scan.

For each doubtful code, the computer therefore tries every variant that changes a single letter through one of these mechanisms, keeps those that give a valid syllable, and ranks them by what the language model thinks of them (the number shown is the gain in log-likelihood: the larger it is, the more Chinese the variant "sounds" at this point). These are leads, not corrections: the text shown is never changed.

The case that validates the method

Telegram 0416b (19 July) ends with a meaningless code, 4210. The same day, its twin 0416a, sent through the other channel, ends with 伸皓, "Shen, the 19th". Searching for a single-letter error finds exactly that: xifa instead of xefa. It is both a plausible Morse error (·· instead of ·) and a copying confusion (i/e). The correction 皓 then reads without difficulty.

Conversely, telegram 0386 ends with 卄 ("20") while the form says 30 June. Getting 卅 ("30") would require changing a p into an n: neither a one-sign Morse error nor a recorded copying confusion. The discrepancy remains unexplained.

A readable version, and its limits

Under each telegram we propose a readable version: the decoded text, punctuated, where some unlikely characters are replaced by the character we believe was intended. Each replacement combines three kinds of evidence: the meaning of the sentence (and telegraphic formulas: 乞速電催, 甚感, 盼復…), the context (other telegrams, known facts, the structure of the message, the date convention) and the closeness of the letters: how many letters must change, and whether that change is explained by a Morse error or a copying confusion.

誠 probable: one letter away, much better sense or external evidence 君 possible: two letters, or a single clue 襯 doubtful, not restored

Hover over a character to see the decoded character, the letters changed and the reasons. Example: in 0406, "段無組織內閣之誕意" means nothing; "之誠意" ("the sincere will") is one letter away (u instead of a, ·· − instead of · −). In the same sentence, 放廈 becomes 政府 ("the government"), each time by a single letter.

Status on the image. Each proposal also states what the re-reading of the forms says: the proposed letter was read (confirmed); or the decoded letter was re-read and maintained, in which case the error, if there is one, dates from 1916 (Morse transmission or Japanese copyist); or the check is in progress, in a blind reading where the reader knows neither which letter is targeted nor what we propose. Message 0406, the held-out test of the key, is never re-read in a targeted way.

Limits

  • These are reading proposals, made by us (Brice Dudout and Claude) from the meaning; they have not been checked one by one against the forms, and the decoded text is never changed.
  • Telegraphic Chinese is extremely terse: several restorations may be equally plausible. We give only one.
  • A one-letter error "explains" a lot: with 26 letters and 4 positions, a code has dozens of neighbours. Closeness is only an argument when meaning and context point the same way.
  • The likelihood gain shown for each message necessarily goes up, since we chose plausible characters: it is an indicator, not a proof.
  • Fourteen characters remain doubtful with no proposal; some passages (0386, the end of 0406, the start of 0498) stay obscure even after restoration.
